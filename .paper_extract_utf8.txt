This article introduces an effective face PAD algorithm based on multiscale perceptual image quality assessment
features. Unique hand-crafted texture features extracted from face images are exploited for spooﬁng detection.
The proposed features are classiﬁed into three major models: generalized Gaussian density-based, asymmetric
generalized Gaussian density-based, and top gradient similarity deviation features. In light of the essential attri-
butes of these models, a total of 21 multiscale features are acquired for classiﬁcation, which is performed through
a support vector machine (SVM). Extensive experiments on ﬁve benchmark databases, CASIA, Replay-Attack,
UVAD, OULU-NPU, and SiW along with our new dataset demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed frame-
work. Experimental results indicated that our face PAD algorithm produced satisfactory detection accuracy on
the tested datasets based on both intra-dataset and cross-dataset protocols. While outperforming a number of
traditional face PAD methods, the proposed scheme achieved comparable results with many state-of-the-art
deep learning-based networks. The introduction of the image quality assessment features with multiscale anal-
ysis into face PAD is promising for detection accuracy improvement.

1. Introduction
Over the past decades, face recognition has drawn considerable at-
tention in image processing, computer vision, pattern recognition, and
biometrics related research. Signiﬁcant progress has been made in re-
cent years and current face recognition systems have attained a high
level of maturity. A traditional face recognition system may achieve a
high accuracy rate but is incapable of distinguishing genuine faces
from spooﬁng faces, which belongs to a ﬁeld with increasing interest
called face presentation attack detection (PAD). As demonstrated in
Fig. 1 , a contemporary face biometric system intrinsically consists of
three major components: face detection, face presentation attack detec-
tion, and face recognition. While the objective of face recognition is to
identify a speci ﬁc person among the massive database, the intention
of face PAD is to prevent attack from intrusion using any spooﬁng face.
Thanks to remarkable advances in modern technology, face PAD has
been playing an important role in the biometric domain for protecting
authentication systems. As can be foreseen, for those authentication
systems without face PAD mechanisms there will be a high chance of
being invaded. This is mainly because that it is relatively easier and sim-
pler to create threatening duplicates of faces in many kinds. For
example, the attacks of face recognition systems [ 1,2] using printed
face photos (printing attack), recorded videos (replay attack), and
even 3D mask models with different expressions (mask attack) [ 3].
However, 3D mask attack is more expensive and complicated so that
2D static attacks of face photographs have been extensively employed
by hackers. Another reason is that abundant facial traits are freely avail-
able on public websites and social networks such as Facebook, Youtube,
Flickr, and Instagram. This kind of intrusion is often in the form of
presenting a counterfeit of an authorized member either with a 2D
printed face photo image or displaying the face with an electronic
screen (e.g., smart phones, tablets).
To address these attacks, abundant anti-spooﬁng security methods
and devices for face recognition systems have been proposed in the lit-
erature. The evolution of the established standards demonstrates the
importance to successfully detect face presentation attacks in real-life
scenarios. Existing methods can be broadly classiﬁed into motion, tex-
ture, distortion, and deep learning approaches. Motion-based methods
generally refer to extracting motion features such as eye blinking, lip
motion, and head movement for spoo ﬁng detection. Texture-based
techniques focus on exploiting texture descriptors, e.g., local binary pat-
tern (LBP), as discriminative attributes. Distortion methods are mainly
based on the measure of sensitive distortion characters. Deep learning
schemes aim at learning the feature representation with deep neural
networks [4,5]. In this article, a new face PAD algorithm for detecting

The philosophy underlying the proposed algorithm is in light of the
fact that synthetic face samples usually contain certain artifacts, which
are inevitably generated during the recapturing and manufacturing pro-
cess. Herein, two types of signiﬁcant artifacts are observed:
• Luminance artifact: Recaptured videos/images tend to present differ-
ent distributions of illumination from the original videos/images.
This is often caused by the incomplete color reproduction ability of
the display media.
• Distortion artifact: During the recapturing process, the detailed infor-
mation of facial samples is reduced and the blurring effect appears
due to re-quantization of the original samples.
The observation of these two artifacts motivates us to take advan-
tage of the deterioration measure of the facial characteristics. For the lu-
minance artifact, we introduce luminance quality-aware features to
characterize structure and contrast deformation. For the distortion arti-
fact, we propose gradient magnitude similarity features, which are sen-
sitive to artifacts generated by compression and blurriness. The
proposed features are well able to distinguish recaptured face presenta-
tion attacks from genuine face images.
Through the analysis of quality traits between genuine and spooﬁng
face images, we investigate a multiscale feature descriptor, into which
various image quality assessments are combined. We provide an in-
depth examination into the usage of multiscale image quality assess-
ment and comprehensive explanation. A number of new facial attri-
butes are exploited including the local face trait, local face trait
product, top gradient similarity, and top gradient similarity deviation
models. In addition to ﬁve existing benchmark databases, we establish
a new dataset that emphasizes the de-focus effect in spoo ﬁng face im-
ages. The main contributions of the current work are summarized as fol-
lows:
1. Usage of multiscale image quality assessment features is attempted
for face PAD. Three different maps of the local face trait, local face
trait product, and top gradient similarity that derive from face images
are uniquely developed.
2. Statistical models of the generalized Gaussian density and asymmet-
ric generalized Gaussian density functions along with a new top gra-
dient similarity deviation model are introduced to acquire essential
features.
3. A new face anti-attack dataset, named CBEL, is constructed that al-
lows for the ability evaluation of face spoof detection algorithms
with emphasis on the defocus effect.
4. Six benchmark databases including ﬁve public datasets of CASIA [6],
Replay-Attack [7], UVAD [ 8], OULU-NPU [ 9], and SiW [ 10], and our
established dataset have been utilized for performance evaluation.
5. Numerous face PAD methods ranging from traditional approaches to
contemporary deep learning-based strategies have been included for
understanding the evolution of face PAD methodology development.
6. Our proposed multiscale hand-crafted texture features provide an
opportunity for the combination of deep learning features for perfor-
mance advancement.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we
deliver a brief review of face PAD related literature. We then describe
the proposed methodology in Section 3. The experimental results are
presented and evaluated in Section 4. Finally, we draw the conclusion
in Section 5.
2. Related work
For the past several years, a wide variety of anti-spoo ﬁng methods
have been proposed to tackle the face PAD problems. Existing anti-
spooﬁng algorithms can be roughly divided into four categories:
motion-based, texture-based, distortion-based, and deep learning-
based approaches.
2.1. Motion-based approaches
Motion-based techniques aim at exploiting motion cues and tempo-
ral information to distinguish the genuine face from the spoo ﬁng one
based on a sequence of video frames. As such, it can be classi ﬁ
ed into
temporal-based and dynamic feature-based methods [ 10,11]. Eye-
blinking detection is a typical motion cue that has been widely adopted
[12,13]. Bao et al. [14] assumed that the test region is a 2D plane so that
planar object movements were detected as cues for translation based on
the optical ﬂow ﬁeld computation. Kollreider et al. [ 15]p r o p o s e da
structure tensor strategy to extract the subtle movements of different
facial parts as important features under the assumption that the genu-
ine and spoo ﬁng faces can be distinguished by the movement cues.
Bharadwaj et al. [ 16] introduced an Eulerian motion magni ﬁcation
scheme to amplify subtle facial motions in a specialized frequency

band by combining two types of feature extraction descriptors of the
LBP and histogram of oriented optical ﬂow (HOOF).
Alternatively, based on foreground/background motion information,
Anjos et al. [17] computed the opticalﬂow correlation between the fore-
ground head and the background scene to perform face photo PAD. Kim
et al. [18] proposed a motion and similarity-based spooﬁng detection al-
gorithm by assuming that the foreground region has low consistency
with the background (a region without a face and upper body). The mo-
tion in the foreground region was then compared with the background
motion index. Although motion-based methods have demonstrated
their effectiveness for face PAD, a certain degree of user cooperation is
necessary to warrant the authentication such as eye blinking according
to the system's instruction. A deﬁned face/scene motion-based model is
usually not applicable to a wide variety of authentication environments.
2.2. Texture-based approaches
Since image texture information has been extensively employed in
many image processing and computer vision applications, it is natural
to explore texture properties for face PAD. Many prior studies exploited
hand-crafted texture features such as LBP [19,20], difference of Gaussian
(DoG) [21], scale invariant feature transform (SIFT) [22], and speed-up
robust feature (SURF) [ 23] associated with traditional classi ﬁers such
as support vector machine (SVM) and linear discriminant analysis
(LDA). Moreover, Tirunagari et al. [ 24] applied the dynamic mode de-
composition (DMD) as a general purpose to capture liveness cues and
proposed a classi ﬁcation pipeline consisting of DMD, LBP, and SVM
with a histogram intersection kernel to determine genuineness. Määttä
et al. [ 19] explored image texture features using the LBP for detecting
face spoo ﬁng attacks. This approach focused on how testing images
were inﬂuenced by the luminance and quality. Benlamoudi et al. [ 25]
computed the LBP, local phase quantization, and binarized statistical
image features between successive frames associated with an SVM for
face PAD.
Peng et al. [ 26] introduced a guided scale space, where two texture
features of the guided scale based LBP and local guided binary pattern
were extracted, followed by an SVM classiﬁer. Arashloo et al. [ 27]p r e -
sented a countermeasure to detect spooﬁng attacks in various imaging
conditions. They combined binarized statistical image features on
three orthogonal planes (MBSIF-TOP) with a blur-tolerant descriptor,
which is executed via a kernel fusion function based on the kernel dis-
criminant analysis (KDA). Boulkenafet et al. [ 28] exploited the joint
color-texture information from the luminance and chrominance chan-
nels by extracting low-level features in different color spaces. Chan
et al. [ 29] proposed a face liveness detection method against 2D
spooﬁng attacks using four texture features and 2D structure descrip-
tors with low computational complexity.
Based on the Lambertian model, Tan et al. [ 21] introduced a DoG
scheme to extract frequency disturbance by assuming that a face
image should be more severely distorted if it is captured by the camera
twice. Komulainen et al. [30
] presented a face PAD approach consisting
of upper-body and spoo ﬁng medium detectors that are based on the
histogram of oriented gradients (HOG) descriptors and SVM.
Benlamoudi et al. [ 23] investigated a face representation for a well-
generalizing anti-spooﬁng method by applying Fisher vector encoding
on SURF extracted from different color spaces. Yang et al. [31] proposed
a component-based face PAD approach using a series of steps. The com-
ponents of the face wereﬁrst located followed by low-level feature cod-
ing. Based on the Fisher criterion, high-level face representation was
then derived from pooling the weighted codes.
2.3. Distortion-based approaches
Methods involved in this category are usually inspired by the image
quality difference between genuine and spooﬁng images such as sharp-
ness, smoothness, artifact, and statistical characteristics in some
transform domains. This class is also referred to as image quality-
based methods [ 32]. Patel et al. [ 22] developed a face PAD system for
an Android smartphone by analyzing image distortion based on inten-
sity channels and image regions. They characterized the degradation
by integrating block based LBP along with color moment features.
Pinto et al. [ 33] introduced a technique using visual rhythm analysis
for detecting spoo ﬁng video attacks. A low-pass ﬁlter was conducted
to remove noise and the temporal information of the video was cap-
tured by visual rhythm techniques. Galbally et al. [ 34] exploited 25
image quality assessment metrics covering pixel-based and structure-
based measures as features for learning the classi ﬁer. Wen et al. [ 35]
proposed an efﬁcient face PAD algorithm based on an image distortion
analysis (IDA) feature vector, which consisted of four different features
(specular reﬂection, blurriness, chromatic moment and color diversity).
2.4. Deep learning-based approaches
Thanks to the rapid evolution in artiﬁcial intelligence, deep learning
strategies have been recently exploited for biometric PAD. For example,
Manjani et al. [ 36] proposed a multi-level deep dictionary learning-
based algorithm that facilitated the layer-by-layer training to learn
deep dictionaries followed by an SVM. Atoum et al. [ 37] introduced a
deep learning method by extracting the local features and holistic
depth maps from face images to achieve better detection accuracy. Me-
notti et al. [38] employed two different deep learning approaches toﬁnd
a suitable model and the corresponding weights for biometric spooﬁng
detection. Feng et al. [32] investigated an extendable multi-cue integra-
tion framework for face PAD using a hierarchical neural network that
was capable of combining both image quality and motion cues.
Lucena et al. [ 39] presented a transfer learning technique using a
pre-trained convolutional neural network (CNN) with static features
to recognize face photo, video, or mask attacks. Inspired by the progress
of deep neural network (DNN), Tu and Fang [11] proposed a fully data-
driven ultra-deep model based on transfer learning. Jourabloo et al. [40]
inversely decomposed a spoof face into a spoof noise and a live face and
then utilized the spoof noise for classi ﬁcation, which was based on a
CNN architecture. Liu et al. [ 10] argued the importance of auxiliary su-
pervision to guide the learning toward discriminative and generalizable
cues in light of a CNN-RNN model and estimated rPPG signals. Li et al.
[41] introduced an unsupervised domain adaptation face PAD scheme
to address the real-world scenario that learned the classiﬁer for the tar-
get domain based on training samples in a different source domain.
Recently, Shao et al. [42] found that subtle facial motion could be ad-
equately captured by multiple deep dynamic texture features from a
convolutional layer of a CNN. The authors then proposed a joint discrim-
inative feature learning model to learn deep dynamic textures for 3D
mask face anti-spoo ﬁng. The proposed learning strategy adaptively
weighted the discriminability of the learned features from different spa-
tial regions and channels. Chen et al. [ 43] regarded the face PAD as a
three-way classi ﬁcation problem to distinguish between genuine
faces, spooﬁng faces, and the background. A cascade face spoo ﬁng de-
tector was designed based on an improved region-based CNN (RCNN)
framework associated with a Retinex-based LBP for handling various il-
lumination conditions. Cai et al. [44] described a two-branch framework
based on the CNN and RNN, where a recurrent mechanism is introduced
to learn representations of local information sequentially computed
from the explored sub-patches. Formerly utilized for convolutional
multiscale feature extraction on the EfﬁcientDet detection architecture,
a bi-directional feature pyramid network was employed for face PAD
with deep pixelwise supervision [45].

3. Proposed methodology
Our face PAD algorithm based on multiscale image quality feature
models consists of four major phases: (i) generalized Gaussian
density-based feature, (ii) asymmetric generalized Gaussian density-
based feature, (iii) top gradient similarity deviation feature, and (iv)
multiscale feature classiﬁcation. Fig. 2 illustrates the overall ﬂowchart
of the proposed framework, which consists of two stages: training and
testing.
3.1. Generalized Gaussian density-based feature
After obtaining the input face image in either training or testing
phase, the face region is segmented using face detection techniques
for further processing. Similar to face recognition, a key to an ef ﬁcient
face PAD system is the characteristics derived from the face image
for genuine and spoo ﬁng classi ﬁcation. The image quality evaluation
metrics have long been exploited to quantitatively measure image char-
acters after restoration when the ground truth is unavailable. This has
motivated us to develop appropriate image quality features (IQFs)
that evolved from existing image quality evaluation metrics for
face PAD. Among the diverse image quality measures, the luminance

3.4. Multiscale feature classiﬁcation
Face images are commonly multiscale and distortions caused during
the reproduction process affect spooﬁng image attributes across scales. It
has been shown that incorporating multiscale information into the IQA
scheme achieved better performance in terms of perceptual correlation
[58]. Consequently, the features described in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 are
computed at two different scales: the original image scale and a reduced
resolution that is obtained by lowpass ﬁltering and downsampling the
original image by a factor of two. Table 1 summarizes the features uti-
lized for the discrimination between genuine and spooﬁng face images
in this study. There are 21 perceptual features with 4 from the GGD, 16
from the AGGD, and 1 from the TGSD models, whose implementation
procedures are described inAlgorithms 1, 2, and 3,r e s p e c t i v e l y .
These multiscale features are subsequently arranged to build a feature
descriptor for classiﬁcation. Among the diverse classiﬁers, the SVM is em-

stimulated by previous PAD studies. Other more complicated classiﬁers
or machine learning models can do the same job; however, it is our inten-
tion to adopt a simple classiﬁer to understand the inherent characteristics
of the proposed features. Speciﬁcally, the LibSVM library [60]i se x p l o i t e d

4.1. Databases and protocols
There are six datasets for this study, which are described as follows:
1. CASIA Face Anti-Spooﬁng Dataset [6]: This dataset contains video re-
cordings of genuine and spooﬁng faces. Genuine faces were recorded
based on 50 subjects. Face data were divided into three categories ac-
cording to video quality. The low-quality and normal-quality videos
were captured using a USB camera, whereas the high-quality videos
were taken using a Sony NEX-5 camera.
2. Replay-Attack Dataset [7]: This dataset consists of 1200 short video
recordings of both actual-access and attack attempts of 50 different
individuals. There are 200 valid access videos, 200 printed photo-
based video attacks, 400 mobile-based video attacks, and 400 high-
resolution video attacks.
3. UVAD Dataset[8]: This dataset comprises 808 valid access videos and
16,268 attempted attack videos of 404 different subjects. Seven dif-
ferent devices were utilized to capture the attempted videos with
full HD quality.
4. OULU-NPU Dataset[9]: This dataset includes short video sequences of
real access and attack attempts corresponding to 55 subjects of 15

nation conditions and background scenes. The videos were recorded

5. SiW Dataset [10]: This dataset provides live and spoof 30 fps videos
from 165 subjects, each of which has 8 live and 20 spoof videos lead-
ing to 4620 videos in total. Four sessions with various capturing sce-
narios were adopted to collect the videos captured with two high-

4.2. Evaluation metrics and parameter sensitivity
A number of evaluation metrics have been utilized to assess the face
PAD performance such as the false acceptance rate (FAR) and false re-
jection rate (FRR). The FAR is a measure for the number of spoo ﬁng
faces misclassi ﬁed as genuine, whereas the FRR is a measure for the
number of genuine images misclassi ﬁed as spoo ﬁng. More objective
measures are the low half total error rate (HTER), which is de ﬁned as
HTER = (FAR + FRR)/2, and the equal error rate (EER). The following
metrics are also utilized: attack presentation classi ﬁcation error rate
(APCER) [61], bona ﬁde presentation classiﬁcation error rate (BPCER)
[61], and ACER = (APCER + BPCER)/2 [ 61]. The smaller the described
metrics the better the detection performance.
There are two essential parameters that may affect the performance
in the proposed algorithm, i.e., the standard deviationσ
s for generating
the blurred face image and the top Ψ percentage for deciding the
threshold in Eq. (36). A variety of different σs and Ψ combinations
have been experimented to understand the sensitivity of these two
parameters. Table 2 presents the face PAD performance in terms of the
HTER using four σs values and ﬁve Ψ numbers on the CBEL dataset.
Roughly speaking, for the same Ψ setting, the greater the σs value the
lower the HTER score. It was when σs = 0.6 and Ψ = 5 that produced
the smallest HTER score of 5.52% among all settings. Similar results
were obtained on other databases such as the Replay-Attack dataset
with the same σs and Ψ setting for achieving the best performance.

4.4. Cross-dataset performance evaluation
We have validated our model in the intra-dataset experiments;
however, there is no demonstration of the ef ﬁcacy of face PAD on un-
known datasets. For completeness, we assess the generalized perfor-
mance of our MIQF + SVM algorithm by training on one dataset and
testing on other datasets. Table 9 summarizes the cross-dataset perfor-
mance evaluation of the proposed technique using the Replay-Attack,
CASIA, and UVAD datasets. It was observed that the experiments with
the Replay-Attack dataset for training produced the lowest average
HTER score of 43.82% on other two testing datasets. This is probably be-
cause that the Replay-Attack dataset offers more diverse face image
types such as printed, phone, and tablet attack videos. Thanks to the de-
signed MIQF in our framework, these variations in the Replay-Attack
dataset were appropriately resolved in the training phase, which in
turn demonstrated robust cross-dataset detection abilities.
In Table 10, we compared our cross-dataset detection results with the
state-of-the-art face PAD methodsin terms of the HTER and its average
on the CASIA and Replay-Attack datasets. The proposed framework ex-
hibited quite close HTER scores of 39.0% and 38.1% for both scenarios,
which were also observed in the Auxiliary method (within 1% differ-
ence). It was apparent that the deep learning-based networks exhibited
overwhelming performance over the traditional classiﬁcation methods.
This was primarily because these multilayer network architectures gen-
erated a wide variety of deep features, which provide substantial insights
for stronger discrimination between genuine and spoo ﬁng faces than
hand-crafted features. Moreover, the inherent feature extraction struc-
tures associated with multiscale resolutions transmit essential face tex-
tures layer by layer in the network, leading to more elegant features
for differentiation than the conventional approaches. Nevertheless, our
proposed MIQF + SVM scheme outperformed other classical techniques
with the SVM and LDA classiﬁers based on the average HTER evaluation.
As techniques for face anti-spoo ﬁng are highly sensitive to the
datasets being adopted, we have evaluated our algorithm using six dif-
ferent datasets, which has been rarely attempted by other researchers.
The presented performance evaluation scores by other methods were
directly from their reports in the corresponding papers. Based on the ex-
perimental results, no method achieved the best performance in every
protocol and dataset. For intra-dataset evaluation with relatively small
data sizes such as CBEL, CASIA, UVAD, and Replay-Attack, the illumina-
tion and texture variation among different subjects is not signi ﬁcant.
As such, our face PAD scheme exhibited quite satisfactory detection ac-
curacy, which implies that two levels of multiscale image quality fea-
tures associated with SVM are capable of distinguishing between
genuine and spooﬁng face images. It may reinforce the judgment ability
if the number of multiscale features is increased, say, 4 times larger, for
achieving better face PAD performance.
On the other hand, for more complicated attack scenarios the perfor-
mance of our method deteriorated sharply comparing to contemporary
deep learning-based approaches. For example, the ACER score produced
by our MIQF+SVM technique was considerably higher than the Auxil-
iary method on Protocol 5C inTable 8. Similar performance degradation
was observed on the cross-dataset experiments in Table 10, where our
average HTER score was approximately 10% larger than the best result.
This was mainly because the 21 proposed hand-crafted features were
not able to efﬁciently capture the different presentation attack formats
between the training (print) and testing (display) data, and vice versa.
With a limited number and formation of texture features, our algorithm
exhibited deﬁcient learning abilities to distinguish between two differ-
ent datasets with distinct face images. Nevertheless, the proposed
scheme strikes a good compromise between detection performance
and architecture complexity. To further improve the detection perfor-
mance of heterogeneous face presentation attacks, one possible way is
to incorporate complementary texture features with multiscale resolu-
tions into our existing model. In addition, it is worth investigating the
combination of the proposed multiscale image quality texture features
with deep learning features for performance advancement in the future.

5. Conclusions
We have proposed an effective algorithm to address face PAD
based on multiscale image quality assessment features associated with
the SVM classi ﬁer, which is called MIQF + SVM in this article. This
MIQF + SVM framework developed the LFT, LFTP, and TGS maps and
exploited the GGD, AGGD, and TGSD models to establish 21 various
image quality features for face PAD. Extensive experiments on six
datasets of CASIA, Replay-Attack, UVAD, OULU-NPU, SiW, and CBEL
were conducted to evaluate the capability of the proposed algorithm
in comparison with a wide variety of face PAD methods. Experimental
results indicated that our face PAD system achieved satisfactory
detection accuracy on the tested datasets based on both intra-dataset
and cross-dataset protocols. Overall, the proposed face PAD scheme
outperformed the majority of the traditional SVM-based methods
and produced comparable results with many state-of-the-art deep
learning-based networks. The incorporation of the image quality
assessment features with multiscale analysis into face PAD has been
demonstrated and validated. Distinguishing genuine face images from
spooﬁng face images using more effective feature models associated
with sophisticated neural networks is worth investing in the future.
